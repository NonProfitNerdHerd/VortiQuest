There is a seductive idea circulating through organizations adopting artificial intelligence: if AI can write the code, perhaps we no longer need so many people figuring out what the code should do.

Describe the application. Generate the screens. Connect the database. Launch. Apparently, the only thing standing between an organization and operational excellence was somebody typing fast enough.

Except the difficult part of building useful software has always included something far less glamorous than code: understanding what people actually need, why they need it, what constraints apply, and how to prove the finished system works. AI can accelerate development. It cannot independently establish the full organizational context that makes development worthwhile. That makes business analysis more consequential—not less.

## A Request Is the Beginning of a Requirement

Consider a seemingly straightforward request: “We need an automated approval process.” An AI development tool can take that sentence and start producing something plausible. A submission form. An approval queue. Notifications. A dashboard. Perhaps even an attractive chart showing how efficiently everyone is approving things.

But who has authority to approve what? Does that authority change based on dollar amount, department, funding source, or the person submitting the request? Can someone approve their own submission? What happens when the approver is absent? Does changing an approved request invalidate the approval? Which exceptions are legitimate, and which are merely habits nobody has challenged?

Those answers determine whether the software supports the business. A working approval button proves that someone built a working approval button. It does not prove the organization has a valid approval process.

The analyst’s job is to turn an initial request into an understood, agreed, testable set of requirements. That involves questions, observation, disagreement, clarification, and decisions. You know: the work that rarely appears in the demonstration video.

## AI Can Work With Context. It Cannot Assume the Context Is Complete.

AI can analyze documents, compare policies, identify contradictions, and suggest questions a team has overlooked. Given access to relevant information, it can be a powerful analytical assistant.

But it cannot simply know that the official procedure is three years out of date. It cannot assume that the spreadsheet used by one employee contains the only functioning record of an important exception. It cannot independently establish whether the person requesting a feature has the authority to define the policy behind it. And it cannot treat silence as stakeholder agreement.

Organizational knowledge is scattered across documents, conversations, routines, relationships, and workarounds. Some of it is contradictory. Some of it has never been written down. Some of it is wrong. A model may fill an information gap with a reasonable assumption. In a business process, a reasonable assumption can still be an unauthorized decision.

The human analyst investigates those gaps, brings the right people into the discussion, and makes assumptions visible before they become software behavior. **A convincing answer is not the same thing as a validated requirement.**

## Automating a Broken Process Gives You a Faster Broken Process

Before asking how AI can automate a process, someone needs to ask whether the process deserves to survive in its current form. Why does the same information get entered three times? Why are five people approving a routine request? Why does a report exist if nobody uses it to make a decision?

Sometimes the answer involves a legitimate control. Sometimes it involves a system limitation. Sometimes it is, “That is how we have always done it,” dressed in business attire. Business process analysis separates those possibilities.

An analyst examines the current workflow, identifies the actual business objective, and helps design a better future process. That may lead to automation. It may also lead to removing a step, clarifying ownership, changing a policy, or using a tool the organization already owns.

The ability to build something quickly does not establish a reason to build it. Organizations need someone willing to say that before the development budget becomes a monument to an unnecessary process.

## Faster Development Raises the Stakes for Getting Requirements Right

AI can reduce the effort required to turn an instruction into an implementation. That creates an opportunity—and a management problem. An unclear requirement can now become functioning software before anyone has resolved the ambiguity.

A misunderstanding about permissions can spread across multiple screens. An incorrect business rule can shape the database, reports, notifications, and integrations. By the time stakeholders see the result, the assumption may already be embedded throughout the application. Speed makes early clarification more valuable.

The analyst provides that clarification by establishing:

- The business problem and intended outcome.
- The people affected and the authority behind decisions.
- The rules, exceptions, data needs, and access boundaries.
- The conditions under which the solution will be accepted.

These are development inputs. Without them, faster implementation can simply produce more rework sooner.

## Testing Must Prove the Business Requirement

One of the most important responsibilities in AI-assisted development is making sure requirements come with a way to verify them. “The approval workflow works” is an opinion until someone defines what “works” means.

For example: “Requests above the established threshold must receive approval from an authorized manager before processing.”

That requirement needs more than a successful demonstration. It needs tests covering requests below, at, and above the threshold. It needs evidence that unauthorized users cannot approve them. It needs to address self-approval, delegated authority, missing approvers, and changes made after approval.

The analyst helps connect each business requirement to clear acceptance criteria and appropriate verification. Developers and testers then help determine how to implement and exercise those checks. Business stakeholders confirm whether the resulting behavior meets the operational need. This is shared work. The analyst makes sure the business intent remains visible throughout it.

AI can help generate test cases and automated tests. But if the requirement is wrong, those tests may faithfully confirm the wrong behavior. **A passing test suite can demonstrate consistency with an assumption. Someone still has to validate the assumption.**

## Human Involvement Must Include Decision-Making Authority

“Keep a human in the loop” sounds reassuring. It means very little if that human lacks the information, time, or authority to challenge the result.

Meaningful human involvement includes interviewing the people doing the work, resolving conflicting requirements, confirming policy with accountable owners, and observing whether users can complete real tasks.

It includes asking uncomfortable questions: Who will maintain this? What happens when it fails? Who can correct a bad decision? Does the proposed efficiency simply move more work onto another department?

The analyst does not replace the stakeholders who own those decisions. The analyst makes the decisions explicit, documents the reasoning, and ensures unresolved questions reach the people authorized to answer them. A person clicking “approve” at the end of a demonstration is not a substitute for that work.

## The Analyst’s Role Evolves With the Tools

Business analysts should use AI. They can use it to organize interview notes, draft process models, compare requirements, explore exceptions, and propose acceptance criteria.

But their value cannot rest on producing documents that AI can also draft. Their value rests on judgment: identifying missing voices, challenging unsupported assumptions, distinguishing a preference from a requirement, and obtaining evidence that a proposed solution addresses the actual problem.

AI-assisted development can take on more implementation work once those decisions are sufficiently clear. Analysts remain involved as prototypes expose new questions, requirements change, and testing reveals gaps. The relationship is iterative. Business understanding guides development; working software gives people something concrete to evaluate; their feedback improves the requirements.

## Building Faster Does Not Tell You Whether You Built the Right Thing

The promise of AI development is substantial. Organizations can explore ideas, create prototypes, and implement useful capabilities with less effort. But generating software does not settle questions of purpose, authority, process, or acceptance. Those questions still require people who understand the organization—and people willing to investigate what they do not yet understand.

As implementation becomes faster, the discipline of business analysis becomes more valuable because it directs that speed toward a defensible outcome. Otherwise, the organization may achieve something remarkable: a beautifully functioning application, delivered ahead of schedule, solving the wrong problem.

And now it has a dashboard.
