Ever had one of those people in your life who asks what you think, interrupts your answer, and then explains why their idea was better before you finished speaking? The person whose mistakes require context, whose opinions require no evidence, and whose schedule is apparently more important than yours? Now imagine giving that person access to your business systems and paying them by the hour. Welcome to a bad developer relationship.

A customer and developer need to trust each other. You should feel comfortable asking questions, raising concerns, and admitting you do not understand something. You should never have to wonder whether your developer is manipulating results, concealing shortcuts, or recommending a solution because it benefits them at your expense. Their ethics should be dependable enough that you can concentrate on running your organization.

Good developers welcome that responsibility. Bad ones would prefer you admire the dashboard and approve the invoice. Here is how to tell the difference.

## 1. They Start Building Before They Understand the Problem

You ask for a volunteer registration form. They build one. Nobody asks whether minors need parental permission, who approves applications, or how the coordinator receives them. Three weeks later, you have forty registrations sitting in a database nobody knows how to access. But the submit button changes color when you hover over it. A triumph.

If your developer sells you a complete solution but shows little interest in how your organization operates, pay attention. They should ask about the people, decisions, and handoffs the software needs to support. The amount of discovery should fit the project, and responsibilities should be clear, but “I just built what you asked for” is a weak excuse from someone hired to help solve the problem.

You deserve a partner who helps clarify your needs before turning assumptions into expensive software.

## 2. They Tell You Design Doesn’t Matter Because “It Works”

You explain that employees cannot find the customer record. Your developer explains that the search function works. Technically, yes—after opening three menus, selecting an unexplained filter, and knowing that customers are called “entities.” Apparently, the receptionist should have completed an archaeological expedition before answering the phone.

Design affects whether people can understand and use what you purchased. Clear navigation, readable text, accessible controls, sensible layouts, and useful error messages are part of delivering a working application. If a customer loses twenty minutes of information because a phone number contained parentheses, “they should follow the format” is an embarrassing response.

You can reasonably expect your developer to take usability concerns seriously. A toilet in the middle of a conference room is functional. That does not conclude the design discussion.

## 3. They Dismiss Your Ideas Without Explaining Why

“Could customers save their progress?” you ask. “No. Bad idea.” Why? “That’s not how you do it.” An impressive technical argument, provided we have recently abolished explanations.

A developer should raise objections when an idea creates ethical concerns, exposes private information, undermines accessibility, or introduces reliability and maintenance problems. Those objections protect you. But they should explain the concern in terms you can understand and help identify a workable alternative.

“We should avoid putting personal information in that link because anyone receiving it could access the record” gives you useful guidance. “Trust me, I know better” gives you an attitude to work around. You should be able to explore an idea without being made to feel foolish for asking.

## 4. Their Credentials Keep Replacing Their Answers

“I’ve built systems for much bigger organizations.” Congratulations. We are discussing why our three employees need separate accounts, not nominating you for a lifetime achievement award.

Relevant experience should improve a developer’s judgment. It should help them recognize risks, explain tradeoffs, and ask better questions. It does not automatically make a solution designed for a large enterprise appropriate for a small nonprofit with volunteers and limited support.

Ask how their recommendation fits your organization’s budget, staffing, and daily work. A capable professional can connect their experience to your circumstances. If they repeatedly respond with their résumé, you are entitled to bring the conversation back to the actual question.

## 5. Every Problem Requires the Solution They Already Know

Scheduling? Their platform. Inventory? Their platform. A food pantry website? Somehow, still their platform. The software equivalent of ranch dressing has entered the procurement process.

Reusing tools, templates, and properly licensed code can save you money. Experienced developers should know when existing technology is a good fit. The warning sign appears when they pressure you to abandon important requirements because their preferred solution cannot accommodate them—and never seriously consider alternatives.

You deserve an honest explanation of what is being reused, what requires customization, and where the limitations are. Their familiarity with a tool is useful information. It should not become your obligation to reorganize the business around it.

## 6. They Build Feedback Systems That Congratulate Them

“Tell us how we did,” says the form. “Satisfied” is already selected. You write, “The reports are wrong, and nobody returns my calls,” then submit. Somewhere, a dashboard celebrates another happy customer. Customer service has finally solved its most persistent problem: customers having opinions.

A preselected positive rating can turn an overlooked field into an endorsement. That may result from careless design or deliberate manipulation; either way, it needs correction. The same concern applies to complaint forms that are difficult to find, cancellation paths buried under cheerful nonsense, and success metrics that count opening a record as completing the work.

You should expect feedback to require an intentional response and reporting to reflect meaningful outcomes. When you flag a misleading design, a trustworthy developer will investigate and correct it. Defending the flattering number deserves considerably more scrutiny.

## 7. “I Don’t Know” Means the Question Has Been Abandoned

“I don’t know. Let me research that” is a perfectly good answer when you trust the person saying it to follow through. They investigate, test, explain what they found, and tell you what happens next. That pattern builds confidence because you know an unanswered question has an owner.

The problem is “I don’t know; that integration is weird,” followed by eleven days of silence. When you ask again, you discover the research consisted of hoping the subject would develop the courtesy to disappear.

Your developer cannot know everything. You can reasonably expect them to acknowledge uncertainty, agree on the next step, and keep you informed. Admitting a limitation is professional. Pretending to know—or quietly leaving you to discover the consequences—is the warning sign.

## 8. Their Demonstration Requires Perfect Users

Their sample customer, Test User, has perfect information, flawless internet, excellent eyesight, and no competing responsibilities. Everything works. Your actual customer uploads the wrong document, presses Back, and loses the application. “User error,” announces the developer, apparently encountering human behavior for the first time.

Ask to see what happens when someone submits twice, loses connectivity, enters incomplete information, or needs to correct a mistake. Ask what happens when an approver is absent or an outside service stops responding. These are normal operating conditions.

A responsible developer helps define realistic acceptance criteria and demonstrates the system against them. You should have evidence that the application can survive ordinary work, including the occasional distracted person having an entirely average Tuesday.

## 9. They Improve One Department by Making Another Miserable

The new application saves managers two hours a week. Accounting now spends eight hours reconciling its exports. The developer declares victory. Accounting begins communicating exclusively through clenched teeth.

A project needs someone to examine the whole process: where information originates, who checks it, where it goes next, and who handles exceptions. That person might be a business analyst, a product owner, or a developer with those skills. What matters is that the work happens and that the people affected have a voice.

If your developer never asks about downstream consequences, raise the issue before implementation. You deserve improvements measured across the organization, including the departments that do not attend the sales presentation.

## 10. Their Failure to Listen Becomes Your Additional Charge

You explained that staff would use the application on phones. They deliver something usable only on a large monitor. “Mobile support is additional scope.” You explained that supervisors approve expenses. Employees can approve their own. “That wasn’t specified at the field level.” Fascinating: their misunderstanding has developed a billing rate.

Additional requirements deserve fair discussion and compensation. Customers also need to participate, make decisions, and respect agreed scope. But developers must own missed requirements, unconfirmed assumptions, and work that fails the agreement.

Look for documented decisions and clear acceptance criteria. If ambiguity consistently produces another payment in the developer’s favor, ask for a review against what was agreed. You should not have to finance the same explanation twice.

## 11. Leaving Them Would Require a Rescue Operation

Your domain is in their account. Your hosting is in their account. The documentation is “coming.” Your data export contains forty-seven unlabeled columns and dates formatted like a distress signal. You ask how another provider could take over, and they become offended. Apparently, the exit plan has hurt their feelings.

Ownership arrangements differ between subscription services and commissioned applications, but the terms should be understandable before you commit. Watch for undisclosed dependence: unavailable credentials, unusable exports, missing documentation, and essential knowledge held by someone who takes three weeks to answer an email.

You can value a partnership and still expect a practical transition plan. A developer should be able to explain your options without treating the question as an act of betrayal.

## 12. They Think Being in Demand Excuses How They Treat You

“I’m very busy. People wait months to work with me.” Then perhaps your project deserved an honest discussion about availability before they accepted the deposit.

A full schedule does not excuse dismissiveness, concealed delays, sloppy work, or ignored concerns. Nor does technical talent entitle someone to make you feel grateful for basic courtesy. Agreed response times, candid progress updates, and respectful conversations are reasonable expectations.

And this is where the AI era makes the ego harder to sell. Customers have more ways to explore ideas, create prototypes, and find competing providers. Skilled developers can use those tools too. Someone whose entire argument is “I can produce code you cannot, so tolerate my behavior” is relying on a weaker proposition than they once had.

AI does not eliminate the need for capable developers. It makes judgment, analysis, validation, design, and accountability more valuable. The developer who listens and takes responsibility has plenty to offer. The one delivering familiar code with a side order of contempt should reconsider the business model. Customers have more options, and arrogance remains an exceptionally poor feature.

## You Deserve a Relationship You Can Trust

One mistake does not make someone a bad developer. Neither does an awkward conversation or an honest disagreement. Watch what happens when you raise a concern: do they listen, explain, investigate, and follow through? Or do they dismiss you, blame you, and remind you how fortunate you are to have them?

Ask for clear commitments about the business outcome, agreed requirements, realistic testing, and ownership of unresolved issues. Then judge their actions. You do not need to prove malicious intent to recognize that repeated disregard is damaging the relationship.

You should be able to trust your developer’s ethics while asking serious questions about their work. They should welcome that distinction. Your organization is entrusting them with money, information, staff time, and the ability to operate; honesty and care belong at the center of that responsibility.

If your developer treats those expectations as unreasonable, finding another partner is a sensible business decision. No need to preselect “Satisfied” on the way out.
