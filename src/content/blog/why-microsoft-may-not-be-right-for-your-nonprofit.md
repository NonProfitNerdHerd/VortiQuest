Microsoft’s nonprofit offerings can look like an obvious win: discounted software, familiar applications, professional email, shared documents, and enough additional tools to make a board member announce, “We could run the entire organization on this!”

Possibly. Who is going to configure it? Who is going to secure it? Who is going to teach everyone how to use it? And who answers the phone when the volunteer who built everything becomes unavailable because, inconveniently, they have a life?

Microsoft can be an excellent choice for a nonprofit. Its productivity tools, security capabilities, and centralized administration can deliver substantial value. But a discount does not establish whether the platform fits your organization. That requires understanding how you work, what problems you face, and whether you can sustain the technology after the demonstration ends.

A powerful platform without the capacity to manage it can become an expensive collection of good intentions. Nonprofits already have plenty of those. They usually live in a strategic planning binder.

## The Free Offering Remains. The Benefits You Relied On May Not.

Saying Microsoft still offers free nonprofit licenses is accurate. It also misses the practical issue for organizations that depended on the donated Business Premium package: the remaining free offer does not replace everything they were using.

Microsoft announced on May 14, 2025, that its Microsoft 365 Business Premium and Office 365 E1 grants would end at customers’ next renewals on or after July 1, 2025. That placed the earliest affected renewals less than seven weeks after the announcement. Other organizations had longer, depending on their renewal dates. Microsoft retained its offer of up to 300 donated Business Basic licenses and discounts on paid plans. But a discounted replacement is still a new expense when the previous bill was zero.

The distinction between those plans matters. Business Premium includes desktop Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Outlook, alongside advanced identity controls, device management, and security capabilities. Business Basic provides web and mobile Office applications, but does not include desktop Office or the same security and management package. The discontinued E1 grant was a separate offering and did not include desktop Office.

For a nonprofit relying on donated Business Premium seats, these were not necessarily luxury features or experimental tools somebody hoped to explore someday. They could be the applications staff used to prepare grant proposals, manage spreadsheets, and produce board materials—and the controls an administrator used to protect organizational accounts, computers, and information. Those are compelling reasons to adopt a platform and build daily operations around it.

So “you can still get hundreds of free licenses” does not answer the concern. Hundreds of licenses for a different package do not restore the capabilities your organization depended on. The number looks generous. The operational gap remains.

An organization could now face a choice: pay to preserve those capabilities, accept a different working environment, or spend time and money moving elsewhere. None of those options becomes effortless because the replacement subscription carries a nonprofit discount. A discount is helpful. It is not a budget appropriation.

Consider what that decision can involve: an executive director finding money in an already approved budget, a board evaluating an unexpected recurring commitment, an administrator sorting out who needs which plan, and staff determining whether their existing work can continue under the replacement. Organizations coordinating employees, board members, and volunteers also need to verify licensing eligibility rather than assume everyone qualifies for the same offer.

This is how a licensing announcement can become organizational chaos. The nonprofit did not necessarily decide to expand its technology program. It may simply be trying to keep doing yesterday’s work with yesterday’s capabilities.

We cannot responsibly claim to know Microsoft’s internal cost of providing those benefits. We can recognize that their value to a small nonprofit may be enormous. Desktop Office access and properly configured security tools can support essential operations in an organization with little room to absorb additional spending.

Microsoft is entitled to change its charitable offerings. Nonprofit leaders are equally entitled to question whether that makes the platform a sustainable dependency. Gratitude for past support does not require pretending that its withdrawal has no consequences.

Annual budgets require defensible assumptions. No vendor can promise unchanged pricing forever, which makes contingency planning part of the purchasing decision. **“We’re all in, and we’ll find out what staying here costs next year” is a precarious foundation for spending donor money.**

## Microsoft Offers Real Benefits—When You Can Operate Them

There are legitimate reasons to choose Microsoft beyond a favorable subscription price. Familiar applications can reduce friction for staff. Organizational email and shared documents can improve coordination. Centralized account management can give your nonprofit more control than personal accounts and files scattered across individual computers.

Business Standard and Business Premium include desktop Office applications, which means appropriately licensed users generally do not need a separate purchase of those included applications. That can be worthwhile when staff depend on them daily. But the access is funded through an ongoing subscription, so the recurring expense belongs in your operating budget.

The security benefits can also be substantial. Business Premium includes Entra ID P1 for identity and access controls, Intune for managing devices and applications, and Defender for Business for endpoint protection. Together, these tools can help protect organizational accounts, devices, and information.

But someone must implement and operate them. Devices need appropriate enrollment, policies need configuration, and security findings need attention. Microsoft’s own deployment guidance describes enrollment and configuration as part of implementing device protections.

The subscription provides capabilities. It does not provide the judgment, staff time, or ongoing attention required to use them effectively. Buying security tools and assuming the organization is now secure is an excellent way to purchase confidence without establishing whether it is justified.

## Your Tenant Is an Environment Somebody Has to Run

A Microsoft tenant is your organization’s dedicated environment for its accounts, services, and settings. Signing up establishes that environment. It does not produce a qualified administrator.

Someone needs to manage users, licenses, authentication, administrator access, external sharing, and service settings. Microsoft documents distinct administrative responsibilities across services such as Teams, SharePoint, security, and Power Platform. Being good at Excel does not prepare someone to manage all of them.

Yet nonprofits routinely assign this responsibility to whoever appears most comfortable with a computer. Congratulations, Susan. You connected the projector. You now oversee organizational identity and access management.

That is unfair to Susan, and it is a terrible operating model.

Administration requires expertise, clear ownership, and time. When nobody has those things, the responsibilities accumulate until an employee leaves, an account is compromised, or an important service stops working.

## Permissions Are Where “Just Make It Work” Gets Dangerous

Staff, board members, volunteers, contractors, and outside partners may all need different access. An event volunteer might need a schedule and registration list. That does not mean they should see personnel records, financial documents, or every other program’s files.

Someone must decide where information belongs, who should access it, how sharing works, and when access should end. Those decisions need maintenance as people and responsibilities change.

The shortcut is to open permissions until the complaints stop. Everyone can finally access everything. What a triumph. The locked filing cabinet has been replaced with a folding table in the parking lot.

The opposite failure is an environment so confusing that staff abandon it and return to personal email and attachments. Now your organization pays for a collaboration platform while the actual collaboration happens somewhere else.

Good administration makes appropriate access practical. That takes more than handing someone an administrator account and hoping they develop judgment through exposure.

## Onboarding Takes More Than Sending a Login

Creating an account is one step. Helping someone work effectively is a larger undertaking.

People need to know where documents belong, how to find the current version, which tool to use, and how to share information appropriately. They need help with authentication, shared resources, and the distinction between personal and organizational accounts.

Without guidance, documents can end up spread across individual storage, shared sites, email attachments, and someone’s desktop. Every location contains a file called “FINAL,” which is how you know the governance strategy is thriving.

Moving into Microsoft also requires decisions about what to migrate, what to clean up, who owns shared resources, and how to verify that people can do their jobs afterward. An outside provider can help, but staff still need to contribute their knowledge and learn the resulting system.

If the implementation budget covers licenses and nothing else, it does not cover implementation.

## Having Power Apps Does Not Mean You Have an App

Power Apps and Power Automate can support valuable applications, approvals, notifications, and workflows. They can also become tools your organization technically possesses but never puts to meaningful use.

Someone still needs to understand the process, organize the data, build the solution, configure permissions, test failures, document the work, and maintain it. A friendly design interface does not remove those responsibilities.

Licensing also requires attention. Certain Microsoft 365 subscriptions include limited Power Platform rights, while broader capabilities and premium scenarios can require additional licensing. A tool appearing in your account does not mean every feature shown in a tutorial is covered.

Before purchasing additional capabilities, ask: **Who will build this, who will support it, and what measurable problem will it solve?**

If the answer is “we were hoping somebody would figure it out,” you have identified a staffing gap. Buying more licenses will not fill it.

A nonprofit processing a high volume of complex requests might benefit enormously from automation. Another organization handling a few straightforward requests each month might create more work by building and maintaining a custom workflow. The value depends on the problem and the resources required to solve it.

## Your Subscription May Be Funding Potential Instead of Results

Unused paid licenses are easy to overlook because each monthly charge seems modest. Add enough unnecessary seats, upgrades, and forgotten add-ons, and the organization can spend meaningful money maintaining access to things nobody uses.

You do not need to use every application in a package to justify buying it. A plan may be worthwhile because staff need desktop Office applications or because its security and device management capabilities meet a real need. The waste occurs when nobody can explain why a paid seat, upgrade, or add-on exists—or what benefit it produces.

“We might use it someday” deserves scrutiny when donor money pays the invoice. If you purchased additional licenses for an application nobody built, you are financing an unfinished idea.

Review subscriptions against actual needs and usage. Even donated tools consume time through administration, training, and implementation. Free access does not make staff hours free.

## The Roadmap Keeps Moving. Someone Has to Keep Up.

Cloud services change. Features arrive, interfaces evolve, capabilities retire, and rollout schedules shift. Microsoft provides its Message center to help administrators track changes and prepare their organizations. Monitoring those announcements is another ongoing responsibility.

Someone needs to determine which changes matter, whether staff need guidance, and whether existing processes or custom solutions require attention.

For a well-supported nonprofit, this can be a reasonable part of operating the platform. For an organization already stretched thin, it can become another recurring demand nobody budgeted for.

Your staff came to serve the community. Interpreting a technology vendor’s evolving product strategy probably did not feature prominently in the recruitment brochure.

Software will change regardless of the vendor. What matters is whether your organization can absorb the pace and complexity of the environment it adopts.

## A Competent Advisor Starts With Your Organization

Before recommending Microsoft—or any competing platform—a competent advisor should sit down with you and understand the work. A product demonstration can come later. First, somebody needs to establish whether the product solves a problem worth solving.

That conversation should include these steps:

1. **Understand how your organization works.** What services do you provide? Who does the work? How do staff, volunteers, board members, and partners coordinate? Walk through actual activities and decisions instead of relying solely on an organizational chart.

2. **Identify your largest challenges.** Where do requests stall? What information is hard to find? What causes mistakes, missed deadlines, or frustration? Establish which problems most affect your mission.

3. **Find the tasks consuming disproportionate time and effort.** Look for repeated data entry, spreadsheet reconciliation, document chasing, scheduling, reporting, and approval follow-ups. Estimate how often they happen and how much time they consume.

4. **Determine where improvements would help.** Some problems call for software. Others need a clearer process, a standard form, better training, or a decision about who owns the work. Automating confusion mostly gives you faster confusion.

5. **Review what you already have.** Examine existing subscriptions, devices, accounts, applications, and support arrangements. You may already own something that meets the need—or be paying twice for overlapping capabilities.

6. **Assess security and your capacity to manage it.** What sensitive information do you hold? Who needs access? Who will administer accounts, maintain devices, review permissions, support users, and respond to problems? Include what happens when that person leaves.

7. **Compare realistic options and their full costs.** Include licenses, migration, configuration, training, support, custom development, maintenance, and eventual exit. Evaluate what happens if a grant ends or renewal pricing changes. Explain what each option improves and what it requires from your staff.

8. **Validate the recommendation with real work.** Where practical, pilot a representative process with the people who will use it. Check whether it saves time, improves accuracy, and remains manageable before expanding.

The result should be a recommendation you can understand: these are your needs, these are the options, this is the expected benefit, and this is what operating the solution will require.

An advisor should also be willing to recommend a smaller purchase, a simpler service, or keeping an existing system. If every conversation somehow ends with more licenses, you may be receiving a sales strategy dressed as an assessment.

## Leadership Has to Ask Before It Approves

Microsoft may be the right choice for your nonprofit. Its productivity applications, identity controls, device management, and security capabilities can justify the expense when they meet genuine needs and receive competent support.

It may also be more platform than your organization needs—or more responsibility than it can sustain.

Leadership does not need to become the technical support department. It does need to insist on understanding the problem, the expected improvement, the full cost, and who will operate the solution. A volunteer, employee, or outside partner can provide expertise. Each arrangement still needs time, resources, documentation, and continuity.

Approving a complicated environment while refusing to fund its administration is a management decision with predictable consequences. Calling the resulting struggle a “technology problem” does not transfer responsibility to the employee struggling to hold everything together.

The same applies to financial planning. A donated subscription should prompt a conversation about dependency and future costs. Leaders need to understand whether the organization could preserve essential capabilities if the donation disappeared—and whether another approach would leave it better prepared.

If nobody has asked how your organization works, where it struggles, which tasks consume its time, and what it can realistically maintain, the recommendation is missing its foundation. You have skipped the work needed to determine whether the purchase makes sense.

And if you are the leader approving it, those are questions you should expect someone to answer before the contract gets signed. The person asking about administration, licensing, security, and continuity may be the person preventing next year’s emergency board meeting. Give that scrutiny the attention it deserves.

**Can you explain how this investment will improve your nonprofit’s work, who will keep it operating, and how you will sustain the cost—or are you approving a bill and leaving your staff to invent the justification afterward?**
