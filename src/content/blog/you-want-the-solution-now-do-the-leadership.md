Every organization wants the dashboard, the application, the clean database, the automation that finally releases employees from a spreadsheet apparently designed during a hostage situation. Leadership loves the possibilities: better decisions, less duplication, faster service. Then the analyst requests employee interviews. The developer asks for approved requirements. Someone mentions testing, training, and ongoing maintenance. Suddenly, our champions of innovation would like to know whether innovation could involve considerably less responsibility.

“I don’t understand why we need that. Can’t you just build it?” Of course. You want a reliable system built around your organization’s needs, but investigating those needs feels excessive. Perhaps the developer could absorb your operational model through the conference room furniture.

**If you want a serious solution, you must support the work required to deliver it.** Leadership cannot delegate that responsibility while retaining all the authority.

## “I Don’t Understand” Is the Beginning of Your Homework

Nobody expects an executive sponsor to become a software engineer. You do need to understand the problem being solved, the intended outcomes, the major dependencies, and the decisions your team needs from you. That includes learning why requirements matter, what testing establishes, how unreliable data affects results, and what employees will need to do differently.

“I don’t understand any of this” is a respectable admission—provided the next sentence is, “Help me understand what I need to know.” When the next sentence is, “So let’s skip it,” we have a leadership problem. Your unfamiliarity with an activity does not establish that it is unnecessary. It establishes that you need an explanation. People recover from not knowing things every day, often through the radical intervention known as listening.

A sponsor who refuses to learn the basics while insisting on directing the project creates conditions for failure. Authority does not compensate for a lack of understanding. It gives that lack of understanding a larger budget.

## Sponsorship Requires Your Actual Participation

Approving funding and attending kickoff do not constitute a complete sponsorship strategy. Neither does appearing every three weeks to ask, “Are we still on schedule?” before leaving without resolving the decision that has kept the team off schedule.

Sponsors must make employees available, resolve departmental conflicts, establish decision-making authority, protect agreed priorities, and support adoption. That requires preparation. Read the brief. Attend the decision meeting. Ask questions before approving the recommendation. Understand what your approval commits the organization to doing.

The team should explain technical matters clearly, and you should make a sincere effort to understand them. If every explanation ends with “That’s too technical for me,” but every decision still requires your approval, congratulations: you have positioned yourself as the project’s least informed bottleneck.

## Requirements: Because Guessing Is a Terrible Business Model

Suppose leadership requests a dashboard showing how many people the organization serves. Simple enough. Put the number on the screen, preferably in blue. Then the analyst asks what “served” means. Does someone count when they register, attend an appointment, receive assistance, or complete a program? If three departments assist one person, is that one person served or three service interactions?

Now we discover four definitions, two incompatible spreadsheets, and a director who insists everyone has always agreed. Everyone has simply been allowed to remain separately confident.

The analyst makes those disagreements visible while there is still time to resolve them. Leadership must establish who owns the definition, bring the right people together, and secure a decision. A developer can implement that decision. They should not have to invent it and absorb the blame when departments object. **Skipping requirements forces somebody to make organizational decisions without adequate information or authority.** The resulting application may look excellent. So can an incorrect answer in a sufficiently attractive font.

## Your Employees Cannot Be Too Busy to Participate

One of the great organizational traditions is approving a project to improve employees’ work, then declaring those employees unavailable to explain their work. The team must discover the process without interviews, validate requirements without review, and deliver something everyone recognizes as exactly what they needed. Has anyone considered adding clairvoyance to the procurement requirements?

Participation requires time. Managers must understand that interviews, reviews, and testing are part of the project—not favors employees perform after their regular work is finished. The sponsor must establish that expectation and address capacity conflicts. If staff cannot participate, leadership must adjust scope or schedule. The technical team cannot solve an access problem by working harder at being denied access.

## Testing Is Where Your Promises Meet Evidence

“We’ll let the users test it after launch.” Wonderful. Production has been promoted to laboratory, and your employees have been enrolled in an experiment they did not volunteer for.

Developers must test their implementation. Business representatives must validate that it supports the intended work. Together, they need evidence that calculations are correct, permissions are appropriate, workflows behave as agreed, and foreseeable failures are handled sensibly. A polished demonstration shows that the demonstrated scenario worked. It does not answer what happens when records are incomplete, approvals are rejected, users change roles, or integrations fail.

**Removing time for testing removes opportunities to discover problems before those problems affect people.** Sponsors must understand that consequence before approving the shortcut. A cheerful instruction to “be agile” does not settle the issue. Agility does not mean sprinting past the evidence.

## Changing Your Mind Has a Cost

Leaders can change priorities when new information warrants it. But a new feature may affect data structures, permissions, integrations, documentation, training, and tests. Sponsors must allow the team to assess those effects and revise commitments.

You cannot add requirements every Tuesday, preserve the original deadline, refuse additional resources, and express bewilderment when quality deteriorates. The arithmetic was available throughout the process. When every request becomes an immediate priority, employees eventually prioritize whatever will prevent the next unpleasant meeting. That is an understandable survival strategy and a miserable way to build software.

## Protect the People Who Tell You the Truth

Projects expose contradictory policies, unreliable records, unclear ownership, and critical processes sustained by one employee’s memory. The analyst documenting those weaknesses is providing valuable information. The developer explaining their consequences is giving leadership an opportunity to act.

Punish either person, and the team learns to bring reassurance instead. Status reports become soothing. Unresolved problems become “items we’re monitoring.” Then failure arrives, and leadership asks why nobody raised the issue. They did. You called them negative.

**Sponsors must make it safe to report facts that complicate the plan.** Otherwise, they receive a progressively less accurate picture of the project they claim to oversee.

## Developers: Know When to Walk Away

A sponsor who asks difficult questions can strengthen a project. A sponsor who starts with limited knowledge and actively learns can become an excellent partner. The warning sign is repeated refusal to learn, decide, provide access, support testing, or acknowledge consequences—combined with an unwavering demand for success.

Explain what is missing and connect it to specific outcomes. Offer practical alternatives: a smaller release, a revised schedule, a simpler approach, or a pause until critical decisions are resolved. Document the concerns and escalate appropriately. Give leadership a clear opportunity to correct the situation.

If it repeatedly refuses, declining deployment, withdrawing from the engagement, or seeking reassignment may be the responsible decision. Provide an appropriate handoff and clearly identify unresolved issues. **You should not endorse a system you have been prevented from validating or promise outcomes leadership refuses to support.** Another weekend of heroics will not give you executive authority. Exhaustion does not resolve conflicting business rules.

## Launch Does Not End Leadership’s Responsibility

Someone must own maintenance, access decisions, data quality, training, support, and changes to business rules. Those responsibilities need named owners and realistic resources before delivery. Otherwise, the original developer becomes the permanent support department because they once answered a question on Saturday.

Their generosity keeps the system functioning until they become unavailable. Then everyone discovers that the maintenance plan was one person’s reluctance to say no. A system sustained through repeated personal rescue has an unresolved management problem. Leadership must recognize and address it.

Organizations deserve developers and analysts who explain recommendations, welcome scrutiny, and keep their approach proportionate. Those professionals deserve sponsors who prepare for decisions, provide access, resolve conflicts, and support validation. Faster development—including development assisted by AI—makes informed decisions more urgent. Producing code faster will not settle an argument about what the application should do.

**If you agree to sponsor a project, learn enough to lead it.** Understand the critical work, show up for decisions, and support the people identifying problems while those problems can still be addressed. When you do not understand something, ask for an explanation—and stay for the answer. Wanting the solution is easy. Sponsorship begins when you accept responsibility for what delivering and sustaining it requires.
